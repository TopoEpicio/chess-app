# Chess Repertoire Research — Positional Opening Repertoire

**Scope.** A long-term study guide for a club-level player building a four-opening positional repertoire:

| Role | Opening | Move order |
|---|---|---|
| White | Queen's Gambit | 1.d4 d5 2.c4 |
| White | King's Indian Attack | 1.Nf3 / 2.g3 / 3.Bg2 / 4.O-O / 5.d3 / 6.Nbd2 / 7.e4 |
| Black | Caro-Kann Defence | 1.e4 c6 2.d4 d5 |
| Black | Queen's Gambit Declined | 1.d4 d5 2.c4 e6 |

**Purpose.** Not to catalogue variations for memorisation, but to build a *structural* repertoire: a small number of pawn structures, the plans that follow from them for both sides, and the endgames they produce — so that the player can name the structure in front of them on move 12 and say what the plan is.

## Structure of the report

Parts I–IV each use the **same four-section skeleton**, so a plan described for one opening can be compared directly with its counterpart in another:

- **A. Variations** — main lines, critical sidelines, move orders, transpositions, and an explicit **theoretical load** rating (low / medium / high) for every line, with the low-theory positional options flagged.
- **B. Middlegame Plans** — for *both* sides: piece placement, pawn breaks, piece manoeuvres, strategic goals.
- **C. Common Endgame Structures** — the pawn structures that arise, which pieces get traded and when, which endgames are favourable and which are not, and what to do in them.
- **D. Model Games & Resources** — real, verified annotated model games plus high-quality resources.

Parts V and VI are the analytical spine: how the four openings reinforce one another, how to choose between them, a 16-week prioritised study plan with time estimates, the five pawn structures to master, a master comparison table, a consolidated verified bibliography, and the verification log.

## Research methodology (summary)

1. **Primary sources first, claims traced to the source that owns them.** Publisher catalogues (Gambit Publications, Everyman Chess), library catalogues (OpenLibrary), dated reviews with ISBNs (British Chess News), official encyclopaedic entries (Wikipedia's opening articles and World Championship pages, which carry complete PGNs with ECO codes and commentary), the primary game record (chessgames.com game pages and PGN endpoints, the 365chess.com opening database for statistics), the Wikibook *Chess Opening Theory* pages, and teaching sources (Chess.com lessons and articles, ChessBase's *Winning Academy*, lichess studies).
2. **Model games were verified, not recalled** — players, year, event, result, ECO code and actual opening moves. Where a score could not be retrieved, the ECO code and its defining line are given *and the gap is stated*. Unverifiable games were omitted.
3. **Corrections were made where the evidence demanded, and are logged.** See Section **V.14 — Verification Log**. The short version: verify the ECO code, not the reputation. Six famous attributions turned out to be wrong (e.g. Botvinnik–Capablanca AVRO 1938 is a Nimzo-Indian, not a QGD game; Karpov–Kasparov 1985 Game 11 is a Nimzo-Indian, not a Semi-Slav).
4. **Bibliography at publication-data level** — author, title, publisher, year, and ISBN where the source supplied it. Unverifiable titles were omitted and replaced, not guessed at.
5. **Sources chosen for plans, not theory** — Zlotnik's *Middlegame Manual*, Kmoch's *Pawn Power in Chess*, Nunn's *Understanding Chess Middlegames* / *Understanding Chess Endgames*, Grivas on pawn play, Müller & Lamprecht on pawn endings, Hellsten on strategy, Dvoretsky & Yusupov's *Secrets of Opening Preparation*.
6. **"Theoretical load" is defined precisely** as the depth and volatility of the concrete forcing lines a player must memorise to play the branch without engine help — *not* its overall popularity.
